# General Chemistry I — Lecture 9: Chemical equilibrium

## Slide 1 - Title
Chemical equilibrium. General Chemistry I, first year.

## Slide 2 - Learning goals
- Understand why many reactions do not go to completion.
- Write the expression of the equilibrium constant Kc.
- Use the reaction quotient Q to predict the direction of a reaction.
- Apply Le Châtelier's principle to concentration, pressure and temperature.
- Tell apart what shifts the equilibrium from what only changes the rate.

## Slide 3 - Reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium
A reversible reaction proceeds in both directions: reactants → products (forward reaction) and products → reactants (reverse reaction).
At first the forward reaction is faster; as products form, the reverse reaction speeds up.
Equilibrium is reached when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.
Equilibrium is DYNAMIC: the reactions do not stop, but the concentrations stay constant over time.
Example: N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g) (ammonia synthesis, Haber-Bosch process).

## Slide 4 - The same equilibrium from two directions
Starting from N2 and H2 only, or from NH3 only, at the same temperature the system reaches an equilibrium state with the same ratio between concentrations.
Equilibrium does not depend on "where I start", but on the temperature and the reaction.

## Slide 5 - Rates and concentrations over time
Concentration/time plot: the curves of reactants and products flatten at equilibrium.
Rate/time plot: the forward rate falls, the reverse rate rises, the two curves meet and stay equal.

## Slide 6 - Law of mass action and Kc
For the generic reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD at equilibrium:
Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b)
The concentrations are equilibrium concentrations (mol/L); the exponents are the stoichiometric coefficients.
Pure solids and pure liquids do not appear in the expression.
For N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3: Kc = [NH3]^2 / ([N2][H2]^3).

## Slide 7 - What the value of Kc means
Kc >> 1: products dominate at equilibrium.
Kc << 1: reactants dominate at equilibrium.
Kc depends ONLY on temperature: it does not change if I change concentrations or pressure, or if I add a catalyst.

## Slide 8 - Reaction quotient Q
Q has the same expression as Kc, but with the concentrations at any moment (not necessarily at equilibrium).
- Q < K: there are "too few" products, the reaction proceeds to the right (towards products).
- Q > K: there are "too many" products, the reaction proceeds to the left (towards reactants).
- Q = K: the system is at equilibrium.

## Slide 9 - An example with Q
If I add N2 to a vessel at equilibrium, the denominator of Q grows, so Q becomes smaller than K: the reaction proceeds to the right until Q equals K again.

## Slide 10 - Le Châtelier's principle
If a system at equilibrium is disturbed (concentration, pressure/volume, temperature), the system responds by shifting the equilibrium so as to partly counteract the disturbance.
The principle is a rule to PREDICT the direction of the shift; the quantitative explanation is the comparison between Q and K.
- Adding a reactant: the equilibrium shifts towards products (it consumes part of the added reactant).
- Removing a product: the equilibrium shifts towards products.

## Slide 11 - Pressure and volume
For gas-phase reactions, decreasing the volume (increasing the pressure) shifts the equilibrium towards the side with FEWER moles of gas.
N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3: 4 moles of gas on the left, 2 on the right, so compression favours NH3.
If the moles of gas are equal on both sides, pressure does not shift the equilibrium.
Adding an inert gas at constant volume does not shift the equilibrium.

## Slide 12 - Temperature and the equilibrium constant
Temperature is the only factor that CHANGES the value of K.
It helps to treat heat as a reactant (endothermic reaction, ΔH > 0) or as a product (exothermic reaction, ΔH < 0).
- Exothermic reaction: raising T lowers K and shifts the equilibrium towards reactants.
- Endothermic reaction: raising T raises K and shifts the equilibrium towards products.
Ammonia synthesis is exothermic (ΔH ≈ −92 kJ/mol): at high temperature the NH3 yield is lower.

## Slide 13 - Catalysts and equilibrium
A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward reaction AND of the reverse reaction, by the same amount.
So it speeds up both reactions equally: equilibrium is reached SOONER, but its position does NOT change.
A catalyst does not shift the equilibrium, does not change K and does not increase the equilibrium yield.
In the Haber-Bosch process an iron-based catalyst is used to reach equilibrium sooner, not to get more NH3 at equilibrium.

## Slide 14 - Summary
| Disturbance | Shifts the equilibrium? | Changes K? |
| Concentration | Yes | No |
| Pressure/volume (gases, different moles) | Yes | No |
| Temperature | Yes | Yes |
| Catalyst | No | No |

## Slide 15 - Common exam mistakes
- Saying that at equilibrium "the reactions stop".
- Saying that the catalyst "shifts the equilibrium towards products".
- Saying that increasing pressure changes K.
- Applying Le Châtelier without being able to say WHY (comparison between Q and K).
